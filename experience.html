Researcher, Microsoft Research
Oct '26 – present · Bangalore · Manager: Gopal Srinivasa

I work on evaluating coding agents, and on whether passing the provided tests is real evidence that code is correct. I built a state-coverage tool for C#/.NET on Roslyn. It parses a repository, builds a call graph that includes interface and virtual dispatch, and extracts every externally callable entry point along with its parameters, fields, static state, return values and external side effects. An agentic generator–checker–reviewer loop across two LLM families then abstracts each variable's domain into bounded value classes with executable C# predicates, and derives per-function path diagrams and class-level state machines. Redesigning the prompt around explicit breadth and depth budgets shrank the generated models about 4×.

Runtime instrumentation through Roslyn source rewriting measures value-class, path, state and transition coverage during dotnet test. Fully passing suites reached only 35–40% of input classes, against 83–90% for extended suites, and the tool surfaced real defects such as missing rollback and uncaught exceptions. I also evaluated test adequacy on SWE-bench Verified, EvalPlus and kGym, which shows that passing the provided tests overstates correctness. LLM-generated mocks raised state coverage from 41% to 99% once missed states were fed back. This work, GHOST, is co-authored with G. Srinivasa, S. Mohan, A. Rastogi and A. Prasad, and is under review at ISSTA 2027.

Student Researcher, Google DeepMind (Frontier AI, AQUA team)
Jun – Dec '25 · Bangalore · Manager: Sagar Gubbi · Skip-manager: Aravindan Raghuveer · Mentors: Deepanway Ghosal, Hiroki Furuta

At Google DeepMind I worked in the Frontier AI group (AQUA team) on a tool-use agent built on Gemini 3.5 Pro. The question that kept coming back was how well frontier agents hold up when the world stops looking like their benchmarks. A standard benchmark reuses a fixed set of tasks, so a high score can reflect familiarity with the benchmark rather than a robust skill. I built VISTA-Bench, an adversarial evaluation framework, to test that directly.

VISTA-Bench generates diverse test environments automatically through a symmetric adversarial protocol, so coverage does not depend on hand-written scenarios. It spans more than 300 synthetic mobile environments, and I engineered the evaluation pipelines to run at that scale. I also formalised automated telemetry metrics for three behaviours: compositional generalization (combining skills the agent has only seen separately), behavioural trajectory consistency (taking coherent routes through a task), and recovery from multi-step errors (noticing and repairing a mistake made several steps earlier). Automating this telemetry cut empirical evaluation overhead by 35%.

The results were sobering. Agents that scored above 90% on standard benchmarks lost up to 42% of their success rate under non-stationary covariate distribution shift, so a benchmark score alone says little about behaviour once conditions change. I characterised the main brittleness modes and how performance degrades as the shift grows, rather than reporting a single drop.

We also found a surprise, a multilingual robustness paradox: agents generalised 22% better out of distribution when evaluated in low-resource languages than in high-resource ones. This runs against the usual expectation that low-resource languages are harder, which is why we quantified it with statistical care. I wrote more about the framework in a post on VISTA-Bench.

Research Assistant, Harvard Medical School (Marks Lab)
From Nov '26 · Remote · Advisor: Prof. Debora Marks

The Marks Lab studies how mutations change proteins, and is known for variant-effect models such as EVE and for the ProteinGym benchmark. Every experimental measurement of a variant is slow and costly, so a prediction that can be trusted, and that knows when it cannot be, is worth a great deal. My project asks two practical questions. Do general-purpose language models compete with specialist variant predictors, and can either kind of model be trusted when it reports its own confidence?

I benchmark the language models against the specialist predictors on shared candidate sets, comparing predicted scores with measured mutation effects, so that every model is judged on the same variants.

To test whether confidence stays reliable, I use three progressively harder splits. Unseen variants within a protein test whether a model can generalise inside a protein it already knows. Held-out proteins and families test transfer to new sequence families. Different experimental assays test whether a prediction still holds when what is being measured changes.

I also evaluate calibration on public mutation-effect datasets. The check is whether low-confidence predictions really are less accurate, and whether rejecting them improves accuracy on the predictions that remain, which is what a scientist would need in order to decide which predictions to act on.

In a further experiment I simulate experiment selection with hidden labels. Under a fixed budget of 20 measurements, I test whether choosing variants by model uncertainty picks more informative ones than choosing at random. I discuss this setting more broadly in a post on machine learning for drug discovery.

Research Collaborator, University of Calgary (DENOS Lab)
Oct '26 – present · Remote · Advisor: Prof. Steve Drew · Collaborator: Jiajun Wu

Emergency departments are chronically crowded, and it is risky to test a change in staffing or patient flow on real patients. A simulator lets a hospital ask what would happen first. EDSim: An Agentic Simulator for Emergency Department Operations does this with language-model agents: GPT-4o-mini-driven patient, triage nurse, bedside nurse and physician agents run the full care journey. The paper, co-authored with J. Wu, Prof. S. Drew and the DENOS Lab team, is under review at the ICML 2027 main track.

I work on checking that the simulator can be believed. I compare simulated wait times with historical data from a Level-1 trauma centre, stratified across the five levels of the Canadian Triage and Acuity Scale (CTAS), and the baseline outputs match the historical wait-time distributions.

I also run what-if experiments. In surge experiments over 326 simulated patient visits (187 baseline and 139 surge), a 60% increase in arrival rate raised mean length of stay by 75.4%, from 552 to 968 minutes.

To see where the delay builds up, I analyse each stage of the visit in a replica of a 79-bed, 15-physician department. Under stress, arrival-to-assessment time rose 74.7% and post-disposition boarding, the time patients wait after a decision has been made about them, rose 100.7%. Boarding rose the most, which points to where relief would help first.

Research Intern, IIIT Bangalore (Summer Research Internship Program)
May – Jun '24 · Advisor: Prof. V. Ramasubramanian

Automatic speech recognition needs many hours of transcribed audio, and most of the world’s languages do not have them. My summer at IIIT Bangalore focused on that problem: building recognition systems for low-resource, multilingual settings, so that communities whose languages are underserved are not left out. I used matching networks, a few-shot learning method that classifies a new example by comparing it with a handful of labelled examples, together with self-supervised learning to cope with the scarcity of labelled data.

The core of the work was cross-lingual transfer. wav2vec 2.0 learns representations of speech from raw, unlabelled audio and then needs only a small amount of labelled data to be fine-tuned, which suits low-resource languages. I built on it with alignment-aware modelling that carries what a model has learned in one language over to another. In complex code-switching scenarios, where speakers move between languages within a sentence, this reduced the phoneme error rate, the share of speech sounds recognised wrongly, by 28%.

I also looked at why recognition fails. I analysed phonetic confusion patterns across 15 language pairs and found systematic misalignments in the acoustic feature representations, which propagate under extreme data scarcity, below 100 hours of labelled audio. Knowing where the confusions come from points to where more data or better alignment would help most.

Finally, I showed that self-supervised pretraining can extend speech technology to 11 low-resource languages, and established baselines that future language-specific acoustic modelling can be compared against.

Nanda Town (NANDA, MIT Media Lab)
From Oct '26 · Team led by Prof. Ramesh Raskar

NANDA is the group at the MIT Media Lab, led by Prof. Ramesh Raskar, working on how AI agents built by different developers can work together. For that to be possible, agents need shared conventions for finding each other, proving who they are, deciding whether to trust one another, negotiating, and paying. Nanda Town is an open-source sandbox for building and testing such protocols across twelve layers, including identity, trust, negotiation and payments.

My contribution is a proof of concept made of three parts: a protocol, which defines how agents exchange messages and what rules they follow; a plugin, which connects it to the sandbox; and a test, which checks that agents actually behave as the protocol says. Working in the open means the result can be inspected, run and extended by the rest of the community.

Digital Twin for AR Microsurgery (Blevins Lab, Stanford University · Hassanpour Lab, Dartmouth College)
From Nov '25 · Guided by Dr. Nikolas Blevins and Prof. Saeed Hassanpour

Augmented reality can place a virtual overlay of hidden anatomy on a surgeon's view. The risk is that an overlay looks equally authoritative whether or not it is correctly aligned, and in microsurgery a small misalignment can matter. This project asks when an overlay helps a surgeon make accurate spatial decisions, and when it misleads.

I designed a phantom (a physical stand-in for the anatomy) and a simulation study around that question. It compares three conditions: no overlay, a fixed-confidence overlay that always looks equally certain, and an uncertainty-aware overlay that signals how reliable its alignment is. Each is tested under varied registration error (misalignment between the virtual and real anatomy), occlusion and clutter.

The study measures three outcomes: localization error, unsafe proximity decisions, and confidence calibration, meaning whether the surgeon's confidence matches how accurate they actually are. The aim is to learn whether showing uncertainty leads to better and safer decisions than showing a confident overlay. The work is guided by Dr. Nikolas Blevins at Stanford and Prof. Saeed Hassanpour at Dartmouth.
